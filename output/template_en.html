Case study · Electoral data analysis

The metamorphosis of power in Alfredo Chaves:we no longer live like our parents

From 2004 to 2020, five mayoral elections lost in Alfredo Chaves, Brazil. In 2024, the turnaround: a candidacy defeated in 35 of the 36 voting precincts of 2020 won in all 42 precincts of 2024. Rebuilt entirely from public, official TSE and IBGE data, with an in-house Python, SQL and visualization pipeline.

00 · About the title

We no longer live like our parents

The title is a reference to “Como Nossos Pais” (“Like Our Parents”), by Belchior (1976, immortalized in Elis Regina's voice), one of the most iconic songs in Brazilian popular music. It's about people who insist on living stuck in the past and fail to notice that the new always arrives, sooner or later. Here that theme takes on a literal meaning: the data in this study shows, precinct by precinct, year by year, twenty years of an old political guard that survived five straight elections before finally being replaced. A metamorphosis of power, and, within it, the real distance between my father's defeated candidacy in 2004 and my brother's win two decades later.

00.1 · Twenty years of data

Five losses and the turnaround

Before the 2020→2024 turnaround documented below, this same municipality carries a longer story. From 2004 to 2020, the group behind this candidacy lost five consecutive mayoral elections in Alfredo Chaves, Brazil. In 2004 the candidate was Jorge Gabriel Meneghel, my father. The family and political lineage runs through every cycle since, up to my brother Hugo Luiz in 2024. The chart below is built from the same official TSE data by voting precinct, across all six elections, and shows the vote share closing the gap, cycle by cycle, until 2024.

Full disclosure. Jorge Gabriel Meneghel, the 2004 candidate, is my father. Hugo Luiz, who won in 2024, is my brother, and I directed his campaign that year. The project grew out of that work; the data pipeline came after, to understand what actually moved the result.
01 · Context

One election, same political base

I worked as campaign director in the 2024 municipal election in Alfredo Chaves, Brazil, for the majoritarian mayoral candidacy of Hugo Luiz Picoli Meneghel. I wasn't involved in running the 2020 campaign: I use that year's public data only as a comparison point, to measure the distance between the two elections. That year the slate lost the mayoral race, winning just 1 of the 36 voting precincts that existed at the time; Hugo Luiz had already been elected to the City Council in that same election, but as part of a bloc holding only 3 of 9 seats, a clear minority. In 2024, also coordinating the campaigns of the coalition's other council candidates, the same political base won all 42 voting precincts in the municipality: Hugo Luiz became the youngest mayor in the history of Espírito Santo state, and the slate's bloc grew from 3 to 5 of the 9 seats on the Council, from minority to majority.

This document reconstructs that turnaround entirely from open, official TSE data, without relying on memory, internal campaign spreadsheets, or any non-public record. It's an exercise in technical analysis over a real electoral dataset.

02 · Data source

TSE's Open Electoral Data Repository

Every number in this study comes from extracts of the TSE Open Data Portal (Brazil's Superior Electoral Court), filtered to the municipality of Alfredo Chaves, Espírito Santo (TSE code 56057), electoral zone 012, for the six municipal elections from 2004 to 2024. Table 3.1 (section 03) shows what exists for each year:

  • votacao_secao_YEAR_ES.csv: votes by voting precinct, 2004 to 2024 (from 1,229 rows in 2004 to 2,089 in 2024, after the municipal filter)
  • votacao_candidato_munzona_YEAR_ES.csv: consolidated result per candidate, 2004 to 2024 (45 to 73 records per election, adding mayoral, vice-mayoral and council candidates)
  • votacao_partido_munzona_2020/2024_ES.csv: party-line vote totals, 2020 and 2024 only (9 and 11 records)
  • detalhe_votacao_munzona_YEAR_ES.csv: turnout, abstention, blank/null votes, 2004 to 2024
  • prestacao_de_contas_eleitorais_candidatos_YEAR.csv (revenue and paid expenses, 2004 to 2024): extracted via HTTP range requests from a ~1.3 GB national file, without downloading the whole thing
  • bem_candidato_YEAR_ES.csv: assets declared by candidates, 2008 to 2024
  • perfil_eleitor_secao_YEAR_ES.csv: electorate profile by precinct (gender, age bracket, education), 2008 to 2024
  • consulta_cand_YEAR_ES.csv: candidacy registry, 2004 to 2024 (gender, date of birth, education and occupation; race/color is only recorded from 2016)
Transparency note. The continuity between the two majoritarian candidacies (2020 to 2024) is reconstructed from verifiable coincidences in the official data: the same local party base (Republicanos/PTB), and the fact that the 2024 winner, Hugo Luiz Picoli Meneghel, appears in the 2020 data as a city council candidate elected on the coalition that lost the mayoral race that year. No number was estimated, projected, or invented: where a figure wasn't publicly available, it was left out rather than approximated. The only exception is section 11, whose 2028 scenarios are stated assumptions, calculated in R.

03 · Methodology

Data-processing pipeline

The raw extracts go through five steps before any comparative analysis: a standard ingestion, cleaning, and load pipeline, implemented in Python (pandas), with the analytical layer in SQL (SQLite). The SQLite database holds only the precinct-by-precinct comparison of 2020 and 2024; the six-election series and the financing, assets, profile and IBGE analyses read the filtered CSVs directly in pandas, with the same cleaning rules. The interactive charts illustrating the study are written in TypeScript (React and Recharts) and styled with CSS.

def clean_secao(df, year):
    keep = ['NR_ZONA', 'NR_SECAO', 'CD_CARGO', 'DS_CARGO',
            'NR_VOTAVEL', 'NM_VOTAVEL', 'QT_VOTOS']
    df = df[keep].copy()
    df['NR_SECAO']  = pd.to_numeric(df['NR_SECAO'], errors='coerce').astype('Int64')
    df['QT_VOTOS']  = pd.to_numeric(df['QT_VOTOS'], errors='coerce').fillna(0).astype(int)
    df['NM_VOTAVEL'] = df['NM_VOTAVEL'].str.strip().str.upper()
    df['ANO'] = year
    return df.dropna(subset=['NR_SECAO'])

04 · Key findings

From a broad defeat to a win in every precinct

In 2020, this political base's majoritarian candidacy won just 1 of 36 comparable precincts (precinct 9), taking 37.8% of the valid vote citywide (3,681 votes), against 53.4% for the winning candidate. In 2024, the same base, now under the coalition “Renovação e Juventude” (“Renewal and Youth”; Republicanos / PP / MDB / PSDB‑Cidadania federation), won all 42 precincts in the municipality, with 56.4% of the valid vote (5,779 votes).

Of the 36 comparable precincts, 28 moved from a minority (<50%) to an absolute majority (≥50%) of the valid vote between the two elections, a swing averaging 20.9 percentage points in those precincts.

A different party every election

The consulta_cand data shows the group's candidate never ran under the same party twice in twenty years: PT in 2004, PSDB in 2008, PSD in 2012, PMDB in 2016, Republicanos in 2020, PP in 2024. The ideological spectrum shifted too, from a left-wing party in 2004 to center and right-wing parties from 2012 on, tracking the national realignment of Brazilian politics over the period. It doesn't look like an isolated local strategy choice.

Coalition size, though, doesn't explain the win on its own: in 2016, the candidate ran with the largest coalition of the whole series (9 parties) and still lost, with 48.0% of the vote. In 2020, with the smallest coalition (3 parties), he also lost. The 2024 coalition (5 parties: Republicanos, PP, MDB and the PSDB‑Cidadania federation) is mid-sized within the series, neither the largest nor the smallest.

On the opposing side, the pattern is the reverse. In the five elections this group lost, between 2004 and 2020, the winner was always PSB: Dr. Fernando took 2004, 2008, 2016 and 2020, and Roberto Fiorin, also PSB, took 2012, four terms and two different names under the same party label, twenty years straight. That streak only breaks in 2024, when Rolmar Boteccia tries to keep PSB in power and loses to Hugo Luiz's PP. In other words, while the losing side changed parties every election, the winning side had a stable label for two decades. It was precisely that stability that broke in 2024, along with the result.

Alfredo Chaves has 7 official districts: the seat and six rural districts (Crubixá, Ibitiruí, Matilde, Ribeirão do Cristo, Sagrada Família and São Bento de Urânia). 05 · Legislative performance

The political flip in the City Council

Alfredo Chaves' City Council has 9 seats. In 2020, Hugo Luiz Picoli Meneghel's political base, then a council candidate for PTB on the majoritarian coalition “Mudança com Transparência” (PTB / Republicanos / Patriota), elected only 3 council members against 6 from opposing parties (the coalition that won the mayoral race: PP / PSB / PSDB, among others): a clear minority. In 2024, with campaign coordination extended to every council candidate on the “Renovação e Juventude” coalition (Republicanos / PP / PSDB), the slate's bloc grew to 5 of 9 seats, a simple majority, if a tight one.

Methodological note. Since proportional coalitions (for council seats) were abolished starting in 2020, each candidate runs alone under a single party. Each candidate's “side” was determined by membership in the parties that made up Hugo Luiz's majoritarian coalition each year (PTB/Republicanos/Patriota in 2020; Republicanos/PP/PSDB in 2024). It isn't a formal proportional-slate tie: that hasn't existed in this form since the 2017 electoral reform.
06 · Financing and turnout

What it cost, and who showed up to vote

Data from the TSE's electoral campaign finance filings (revenue and expenses declared by every candidate, opponents included) and its turnout breakdown (attendance and abstention) round out the picture: the turnaround didn't come from a bigger electorate, and the revenue raised by the group's candidacy was only the largest among the competitors in 2024. In the five earlier defeats, the winner declared 1.5 to 4.4 times more revenue than the group's candidate. Even so, the group's campaign converted the money into votes more efficiently than any other candidate for the office in 2008, 2012, 2016, 2020 and 2024; in 2004 it was the second most efficient. The two figures below show every mayoral candidate of the six elections.

Turnout doesn't explain the 2024 turnaround, but it's been falling for twenty years

Between 2020 and 2024, the registered electorate grew just 4.6% (12,294 → 12,860) and turnout stayed practically flat: 79.15% in 2020, 79.74% in 2024. Since the slate's candidate grew 57% in absolute votes (3,681 → 5,779) while the total electorate barely moved, the turnaround was predominantly conversion of voters who already voted, not mobilization of new ones.

Across all six elections, though, turnout isn't flat: it fell steadily, from 91.8% in 2004 to the 79-80% range of 2020 and 2024. The registered electorate grew 39% over that same period (2004: 9,247; 2024: 12,860), but the number of people who actually voted grew far less, 20.8% (2004: 8,492; 2024: 10,255), because a growing share of registered voters stopped showing up.

Source: detalhe_votacao_munzona, TSE, mayoral race, all six elections (2004 to 2024).

Electorate profile: almost nothing changed

The electorate's demographic profile (perfil_eleitor_secao data, TSE) also stayed stable over time. The gender split has been slowly converging toward parity every election: 51.7% men / 48.2% women in 2008, 51.1% / 48.8% in 2012, 50.6% / 49.3% in 2016, 50.2% / 49.7% in 2020, and 49.9% / 50.0% in 2024 (the TSE never published this figure for 2004). Practically fifty-fifty all along, with a steady trend toward a slight female majority.

A data curiosity. The number of voters aged 16 to 24 was exactly 1,225 in 2020 and 1,225 in 2024: same age bracket, same total, two different elections. It's a real coincidence, confirmed row by row in the official TSE database. It has no obvious causal explanation, but it rules out the idea that the turnaround was driven by a wave of newly-registered young voters: the municipality's young-voter base simply neither grew nor shrank between the two elections.

Cross-referencing with IBGE: the electorate grew, the population didn't

To understand whether the electorate's growth (9,247 registered in 2004 → 12,860 in 2024, +39%) reflects the municipality growing, we cross-referenced TSE data with IBGE's annual population estimates (SIDRA, table 6579, municipality code 3200300). The answer is no: Alfredo Chaves' estimated population stayed practically flat over the period (14,113 in 2004, ~14,373 in 2024), with dips in some years. The electorate didn't grow because the municipality grew. It grew because an increasing share of the population already living there became registered to vote.

Sex, race and income: do candidates look like the people who live here?

IBGE's 2022 Demographic Census (SIDRA, tables 9514 and 9605) makes it possible to go beyond total population and cross-reference sex and race/color with who actually runs for office. Alfredo Chaves' population is 49.3% women and 50.7% men, 61.4% white, 34.3% mixed-race (“pardo”) and 4.2% Black, numbers very close to the registered electorate's, which is also moving toward gender parity. Among candidates, though, the picture diverges: of the 69 council candidates in 2020, 37.7% were women; in 2024, out of 70 candidates, that share fell to 32.9%, the opposite of the electorate's trend, which keeps moving closer to 50%. Mayoral candidates are left out of these percentages: there are only three per election, and in both 2020 and 2024 all three were men.

A name behind the number. The Alfredo Chaves City Council's own legislative record (Commendation Motion No. 003/2024, authored by then-council-member and now-mayor Hugo Luiz) honors Regina Puppin as “the first and only Council member of the Municipality of Alfredo Chaves to hold a seat in the Municipal Legislature”, in other words, the only woman ever to hold a Council seat in the municipality's history, to this day. She doesn't appear among any of the council candidates the TSE registered between 2004 and 2024 consulted for this study, which indicates her term predates that period. The gap in female representation on the Council is older and deeper than the six elections analyzed here.

The racial composition of the council candidacies follows a similar pattern. In 2020, white candidates were 59.4% of the total, close to the municipality's population share of 61.4%. By 2024 that share rose to 68.6%, while mixed-race candidates fell from 30.4% to 22.9%: a drift away from Alfredo Chaves' racial composition, even as the share of Black candidates grew slightly (7.2% → 8.6%). Among mayoral candidates, all three in 2020 were white; in 2024, two of the three were white and one was mixed-race.

07 · Digital campaign

What the posts show about the race

This section steps outside the TSE dataset and uses a different source: the post history of the candidacy's official Instagram account (@hugoluiz11_), from the day of party affiliation through the election result. It isn't public, auditable data like the rest of this study. It's the campaign's own archive, reviewed post by post from screenshots exported from the app, with date, views, likes, comments and shares for each publication. We treat these numbers the way the campaign team saw them in real time: a measure of organic reach and engagement, not voting intention.

The timeline covers six milestones: affiliation to Progressistas (PP) on March 27, 2024; the pre-candidacy launch on June 7; the running-mate announcement for Cleber Bianchi, at the August 3 party convention; the public dispute over a suspended poll on September 22; the win on October 6; and the thank-you post for 5,779 votes, published hours after the result: the highest-engagement post of the entire campaign.

We classified every one of the 166 posts identified by content type, to understand not just when reach grew, but what the campaign was actually posting. Street events and walks are the most common format (nearly a third of posts), followed by policy proposals, coalition posts, and routine administrative communication from the council-member term (City Council session notices, which the campaign posted in pairs: an invite beforehand, an explainer video after). But volume isn't the same as engagement: voter testimonials, which appear far less often, engage well above average, more than four times routine administrative communication.

Limits of this data. Unlike the rest of this study, this section doesn't come from a public, auditable repository: it's a manual read of screenshots from the campaign's own post history, without access to the Instagram API or to paid-reach, impression, or audience-demographic metrics. We reviewed all 166 screenshots in both files supplied (90 and 76 pages) and identified 166 distinct posts with legible date, views, likes, comments and shares - a cross-check by like and comment counts confirmed each screenshot maps to exactly one post, with no duplicates. The content-type classification is ours, made from each post's caption and format, not an Instagram-native category. A handful of older administrative posts' dates (April to August) were estimated from their position in the timeline where the capture didn't show one clearly. View and engagement counts are whatever Instagram displayed at the moment of capture, not necessarily each post's final total, which keeps changing as it continues to receive interactions.
08 · Candidate profile

Who the candidates were, beyond the votes

consulta_cand and bem_candidato data (TSE) bring age, education, occupation and declared assets for each candidate: public information, required by law at every election. Table 8.1 gathers the 16 mayoral candidates of the six elections, from 2004 to 2024, and Figures 23 and 24 compare them. The contrast with Hugo Luiz is the sharpest in the entire study.

Hugo Luiz was 25 on election day in 2024, holds a law degree from UFES, and, per the TSE's records, is white and single. The other two mayoral candidates that year were 61 and 71.

The 9 registered election polls

The TSE maintains its own system for registering election polls (pesqele‑divulgacao.tse.jus.br), required under Article 33 of Law 9,504/97. For Alfredo Chaves in 2024, 9 polls are on record, from 5 different firms, between April and September. We opened and read all 9 registration filings in PDF. It's worth noting a real limit of the public data: the TSE doesn't publish the result (voting-intention percentage per candidate) in these filings, only formal confirmation of registration (firm, protocol, date, scope). It's the commissioning firm that releases the result, typically through a local press outlet.

We located the original document for 6 of the 9 polls: the full reports each institute produced for its client, with methodology, sample size and margin of error. Two of them (Apr 16 and Sep 21) had also been released to the press; the other four are real data, but restricted in circulation, never published by a press outlet:

  • Apr 15–16, 2024: Instituto Solução (TSE ES‑09808/2024), 403 respondents, 4.9 p.p. margin, commissioned by AQUI NOTÍCIAS.COM (released).
  • Aug 29–31, 2024: Instituto Veritá (TSE ES‑08806/2024), 352 respondents, 5.5 p.p. margin, commissioned by Marataízes Piúma Iriri Internet Ltda. (not publicly released).
  • Sep 17, 2024: no institute or TSE registration number appears on the document (an atypical result, see the note below).
  • Sep 20–21, 2024: Inove Consultoria (TSE ES‑04825/2024), 574 respondents, 4.0 p.p. margin, commissioned by H.A. Comunicações Ltda. (released by Montanhas Capixabas).
  • Sep 27–29, 2024: Ipopes Pesquisa de Opinião Pública, 400 respondents (not publicly released).
  • Oct 1–2, 2024: I9‑Inove Consultoria (TSE ES‑06358/2024), 373 respondents, 5.0 p.p. margin, commissioned by Portal ES Agora (not publicly released).

The six real-data points don't share one calculation basis: the Aug 29–31 poll and the official Oct 6 result sum to 100% among the three named candidates, but the Sep 20–21, Sep 27–29 and Oct 1–2 polls (the three 3-way polls with a gap) leave 9.6 to 23.75 percentage points unassigned, a sign those three report the share of the total sample (undecided included), not the share among named candidates. We normalize those three to sum to 100% among the names polled. The Apr 16 poll is different: it only had 2 names (Boldrini wasn't a candidate yet), and normalizing a 2-way race would artificially inflate both existing names, so that point appears with its raw value, as reported in the original document.

Data notes. One of the registered polls (Direta Propaganda e Eventos) was obtained, but the document referred to a different municipality (Conceição do Castelo, ES), likely a filing error on the commissioning firm's side, so it wasn't used here. 2 of the 9 registered polls still have no document located. The full biographical profile of 2020 candidates required downloading an entire 85 MB national file (no partial-download support), which worked, but more slowly and less elegantly than the rest of the pipeline.

10 · Conclusion

What the data shows

Reading the results precinct by precinct, and then district by district, shows that the 2024 turnaround closes an arc of twenty years and six elections: from the 2004 candidacy to the 2024 win, two decades later. The group's vote share grew in nearly every cycle, with a single dip in 2020, and the 2024 result wasn't concentrated in a few strongholds: it was spread across nearly the entire electoral territory of the municipality, from the 42 precincts to the 7 districts, with an average gain of more than 20 percentage points in precincts where the majority flipped between 2020 and 2024. That's consistent with a strategy of territorial reach rather than reinforcing already-favorable bases: the one precinct already won in 2020 (precinct 9) saw a relatively modest additional gain (+13.7 p.p.) compared with precincts that started from a loss, and even the district historically most resistant to the group, Ribeirão do Cristo, went from 8.8% in 2004 to 72.3% in 2024.

The expanded campaign coordination in 2024, including for the first time the council candidacies, coincides with a second turnaround, now in the Legislature: the same political base that elected Hugo Luiz to the Council in 2020 with just 3 of 9 seats (an isolated minority) came to control 5 of 9 seats in 2024 (a simple majority), with a 22.7% increase in the combined vote for the slate's candidates. That suggests a coattail effect between the majoritarian and proportional races, though this analysis doesn't isolate causality between the two. What the 2028 scenarios do not do is in section 11; the study's limits and the author's position are in section 12.

11 · Scenarios for 2028

What the numbers allow us to imagine for 2028

This is not a forecast. The series has only six elections, no 2028 candidate is known, and municipal politics changes from one cycle to the next. What follows are “what if” scenarios: each one states the assumption it uses. The calculations were done in R (analysis/cenarios_2028.R), with the same TSE and IBGE data as the rest of the study.

How many votes will be at stake

The electorate grows almost linearly: 9,247 registered voters in 2004 and 12,860 in 2024. Extending that line to 2028 (linear regression with six points), the base scenario is 13,767 registered voters, with an 80% range of 13,091 to 14,443. With turnout and the share of valid votes between the 2020 and 2024 values (79.1% to 79.7% turnout; 94.7% to 97.1% of those present casting valid votes), that gives about 10,486 valid votes (9,810 to 11,180), against 9,955 in 2024.

How much the group can lose and still lead

In Alfredo Chaves the candidate with the most votes wins: there is no runoff in a municipality with fewer than 200,000 voters. So the share needed to lead depends on the third candidate. With no third candidate, the group needs more than 50% of valid votes; with a third candidate at 10%, as in 2024, passing 45% is enough. Starting from 2024's 58.1%, the largest drop the group can withstand is −8.1 p.p. with no third candidate, −10.6 p.p. with a third at 5% and −13.1 p.p. with one at 10%.

As a yardstick, the history itself: between consecutive elections the group's share swung from −8.0 to +18.1 p.p. (mean +6.3; standard deviation 9.8, with only five swings). If the 2028 swing followed that spread around zero, the result would fall between 45.6% and 70.6% in 80% of cases: a range far too wide to call a forecast, and one that includes precisely the 45% to 50% threshold.

And in the districts

Applying the same swing to every district (an unrealistic assumption, but useful to see where the advantage is most fragile), the group keeps more than 50% in 6 of the seven districts with a 5-point drop and in 3 with a 10-point drop. Sede, which holds about two thirds of valid votes, loses its absolute majority between −5 and −10 p.p.: that is where the lead is decided.

What this exercise does not do

  • It does not know who the candidates will be. Nor whether the mayor elected in 2024 will run for re-election, nor how many slates there will be.
  • It assumes a uniform swing. In practice districts swung in very different ways: Ribeirão do Cristo went from 9.4% in 2012 to 58.2% in 2020.
  • It does not model re-election effects, the political climate, or polls. There are six data points; any more sophisticated model would be false precision.

12 · Limitations and bias

What this study cannot show

The study describes what public data shows. There are things the data does not show, and readers should know which ones before drawing conclusions.

What the data does not allow us to conclude

  • Description, not cause. The numbers show where and when the vote changed, not why. The mayoral turnaround, the growth of the council slate and the wider campaign coordination coincide in time, but nothing here isolates the effect of one on another, nor separates the campaign from the political climate, rival candidates or local factors.
  • Precincts and districts, not voters. Votes are counted by precinct and summed by district; no data says how each person voted. The correlations between the vote for mayor and for council (section 05) describe precincts and should not be read as individual behavior. This is the ecological inference problem.
  • One municipality, no comparison. All the data is from Alfredo Chaves. The study does not compare with neighboring municipalities or the state average, so it cannot say how unusual the turnaround is for the region.
  • Districts assigned by polling place. Each precinct's district comes from its 2024 polling place (section 04). Because precincts in this municipality only receive new numbers and none is renumbered, the assignment holds for every year, but it is still an assignment, not a TSE field.
  • Uneven coverage across years. Table 3.1 shows what exists for each election. Where a dataset does not cover a year, the analysis is left out rather than estimated.
  • Short series. There are six elections. That is why the 2028 scenarios (section 11) are stated assumptions, not forecasts.
  • Two sources that cannot be audited in the same way. The candidacy's Instagram posts (section 07) were read from screenshots, and the TSE registers the 2024 polls without publishing their results (section 08).

The author's position

I was the 2024 campaign director, the child of a 2004 candidate and the brother of the candidate elected in 2024. That can tilt what I chose to analyze and how I framed the result. To reduce the risk, I used only public, reproducible data, with no internal campaign spreadsheets or records; I left the code in the repository so anyone can redo the calculations from the same extracts; I kept in the study the results that are unfavorable to the group (the five consecutive defeats from 2004 to 2020); and I used neutral language, “group” and “opposition”. This does not remove the bias. It only makes it checkable.
